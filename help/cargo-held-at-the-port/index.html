Whether cargo held against an invoice is lawful at all

Goods held for non-payment and goods held after payment are different situations under US rules, and 76 of the 187 pages SeaRates measured discuss holding without making that distinction. FMCSA states it plainly: an interstate mover may legally hold your belongings if you have not honoured the contract and paid on time. The same agency says it may take enforcement action when a mover or broker knowingly and wilfully breaks the contract and fails to deliver at the agreed destination after the customer has paid.

Goods held on the ocean leg follow a different set of rules from goods held on a truck, which is why the next section is a map rather than a single address.

Where the free complaint goes, by leg

Cargo held on one leg of a move belongs to a different regulator than cargo held on another, and complaints are not forwarded between them. Sending the right one first saves weeks.

FMCSA names the practices it treats as moving fraud: a deliberately low estimate followed by a demand for more money, withholding belongings until the customer pays above the quote, and knowingly assigning a false weight to a shipment. Each report is kept in the company's record and feeds the decision on whether to investigate; the sender receives a letter about its status.

Ocean cargo falls to the Federal Maritime Commission. Its consumer service handles disputes on international household goods and vehicle shipments, and the FMC requires any company offering ocean carriage of household goods or a car to or from the US to be licensed or registered with it — the list is public at www2.fmc.gov/oti/, and which credential covers the ocean leg explains how to read it. A customs hold running at the same time as a carrier dispute does not pause the terminal's own clock: dwell time and charges at this port accrue independently of whatever the carrier dispute resolves, so a customs delay can still produce a demurrage bill even once the underlying carrier complaint is settled.

Of the 187 pages SeaRates measured in this search result, 17 name any of these channels, 74 name a regulator without saying where to write, and 27 mention a claim deadline. The gap between naming a regulator and naming its intake form is where most readers get stuck.

What the invoice rule can do for you today

An invoice for demurrage or detention carries a shortcut that does not involve a lawyer. Under the FMC rule in force since 28 May 2024, such an invoice must be issued within 30 calendar days of the charge being incurred, must go to the contracting party or the consignee but not both, and loses its force if it omits any required element. The recipient has at least 30 days to dispute it.

Check the charges on the invoice for four things before arguing about the amount: the date the charge arose, the date the invoice was issued, which party it names, and whether the required elements are present. A formal defect is faster to establish than an argument about whether the delay was fair, and the difference between demurrage and detention sets out the mechanics in full.

Demurrage, storage and terminal charges accrue while the dispute runs, which is why the invoice check happens today rather than after the weekend. Demurrage or detention is named by 37 of the 187 pages SeaRates measured, and the 30-day billing rule by far fewer. Free time and storage differ by terminal, and how a specific US port charges for the wait is a separate question from the invoice rule above.

Documents to collect before the first call

Cargo held disputes are won or lost on paperwork that existed before the problem started. Gather these before contacting anyone, because the first conversation goes better when the file is already complete.

  • The bill of lading, and the name of the legal entity that issued it.
  • The signed estimate or quotation, and any revision to it.
  • Every invoice received, with dates of issue.
  • The inventory or packing list, with the condition record from loading.
  • Photographs taken at pickup, and any terminal condition report.
  • The full message thread where charges were discussed.

The entity on the bill of lading matters more than the brand you booked with: it decides which regulator applies and who a claim goes to. If the document names a party you have never heard of, that is the counterparty, and the company you paid may have been a broker.

Nine months — 270 days — is the written-claim window for an interstate move, counted from delivery. The window for ocean cargo claims is set by the bill of lading and is usually shorter — read it today rather than after the goods arrive.

The bond nobody mentions

A shipment held by a licensed ocean intermediary has one more route that 3 of 187 pages mention: a claim against the operator's bond. An NVOCC licensed by the FMC posts a $75,000 bond, and the surety is named in the same registry that shows the licence.

That bond is a shared pool across all claimants rather than insurance on your consignment — in a large failure it divides thinly, and it is not a substitute for cargo insurance. It is still worth knowing about, because filing against a bond costs nothing but the paperwork and it puts pressure where a phone call does not.

Where the counterparty is a mover rather than an ocean intermediary, the equivalent security sits with the licensing authority for that leg. Either way, the first step is the same: identify the licensed entity, then find its surety in the public record. The FMC registry held 9,795 organisations when SeaRates read the official export on 07/28/2026, of which 5,205 carried an active licence number (675 forwarder-only, 1,942 NVOCC-only, 2,588 holding both roles) — the registry's own web search page shows a different, larger count because it tallies trade-name listings rather than organisations.

When to stop escalating and start a claim

Cargo held for weeks stops being an operational problem and becomes a legal one, and the honest answer is that this page cannot tell you when that line is crossed for your shipment. What it can tell you is which deadlines are fixed.

Two are worth writing down now: the 9-month written claim window for an interstate move, and the 30-day minimum dispute window on a demurrage invoice, counted from the date of issue. Everything else — small claims thresholds, limitation periods for suing a carrier, the value of an arbitration clause in the bill of lading — depends on the contract and the state, and needs a lawyer rather than a web page.

SeaRates has not measured how long a complaint to either regulator takes to resolve, or how often it recovers goods or money — and 0 of the 187 pages measured report either figure with a sample behind it. Pages promising a timeline are guessing, and this one will not. Claims of that kind belong in a court filing rather than on a web page. Frequently asked questions

On a non-binding estimate the mover cannot demand more than 110% of it at delivery — on a $6,000 estimate that is $6,600 — and the rest is billed later. 9 months from delivery for an interstate move. For an interstate move, yes — if you have not paid what the contract requires.

Can a moving company legally hold my belongings?

For an interstate move, yes — if you have not paid what the contract requires. FMCSA says so directly. If you have paid in full and the goods are still held, that is the situation the agency describes as enforcement-worthy, and the complaint goes to NCCDB.

What if the bill is higher than the estimate?

On a non-binding estimate the mover cannot demand more than 110% of it at delivery — on a $6,000 estimate that is $6,600 — and the rest is billed later. On a binding estimate the amount is fixed unless a new estimate was issued for added items or services.

Where do I report a freight forwarder holding my cargo?

For the ocean leg, the Federal Maritime Commission's consumer dispute service. Check first that the company appears in the FMC registry — the entity on your bill of lading is the one to name.

How long do I have to file a cargo claim?

9 months from delivery for an interstate move. For ocean cargo the period comes from the bill of lading and is often shorter. Read the document rather than assuming.

Is my container under a customs hold or a carrier hold?

Different parties and different fixes. A customs hold or examination is CBP's; a carrier or terminal hold is commercial and usually about money. Ask which one it is in writing — the answer decides who you talk to next.

Does reporting actually work?

SeaRates has not measured outcomes and will not claim a success rate. What is known is that reports are recorded in the company's FMCSA record and used when deciding whether to investigate, that 17 of 187 pages tell readers the channel exists, and that using it costs $0.
