How to pick a customs broker, and why no ranking of the best one exists

By SeaRates · Updated 29 July 2026 · Every figure here carries its unit, the date it was read and the corpus behind it. Corrections with a source: corrections@searates.top

⚑ What is checkable before you appoint one

A customs broker is licensed by Customs and Border Protection — a different agency and a different licence from the Federal Maritime Commission credential that covers the ocean leg. A company may hold one, both or neither. Entry preparation runs $100 to $250 across the 299 pages SeaRates measured, and the fee is the smallest part of what is at stake: misclassification exposure and ISF liquidated damages both exceed it by an order of magnitude.

Why there is no "best US customs broker" list here

A ranking needs a metric that is measured and published. For customs brokers the candidates are entry accuracy, post-summary correction rate, exam rate and response time — and none of them is published per company by anyone, including the regulator.

What is published is the licence and the permit. That supports a filter, not an ordering, and a filter is what this page offers. Of the 245 pages measured across the ranking clusters, 54 mention customs brokers at all, and none publishes a method for choosing between them.

The fee schedule is where the surprises live. Entry preparation is the headline; classification work, post-summary corrections and exam handling are commonly separate lines.

The bond decision, which the broker will raise

A customs bond guarantees payment of duties and fees. A single-entry bond covers one shipment; a continuous bond covers a year of them and usually costs less past roughly four entries.

The broker often arranges it, and the arrangement is worth understanding: whose bond is being used, what it covers, and what happens to it if you change brokers. A bond in the broker's name is convenient and ties the relationship to it.

Two questions to settle before you appoint one

Who files the ISF, and by when. The Importer Security Filing must be lodged at least 24 hours before the cargo is loaded at the foreign port, and it has carried liquidated damages since it came into force on 26 January 2009. The obligation sits with the importer whoever performs it, so the answer belongs in writing with the deadline attached.

What happens when the classification is wrong. Ask how post-summary corrections are handled and what they cost, because that is the scenario the fee schedule usually does not cover and the one where the money actually is. A broker that has a clear answer has done it before.

The split between what a broker charges and what it merely collects on CBP's behalf is set out line by line in what a customs broker actually costs.

Frequently asked questions

How to pick a customs broker?

Check the CBP permitted brokers list for the licence and the district permit, establish in writing who files the ISF, get the full fee schedule beyond entry preparation, and understand whose customs bond is being used.

Who is the best US customs broker?

No published metric supports an answer. Entry accuracy, correction rates and exam rates are not published per company by anyone, so this site publishes the filter rather than an invented ordering.

Do I need a broker in every port I clear through?

A broker needs a district permit covering where your cargo clears. One licence does not automatically cover every port, which is why the permit is a separate line on the CBP list.

Is a customs broker the same as a freight forwarder?

No. A broker is licensed by CBP to file entries; a forwarder or NVOCC is licensed by the FMC to arrange ocean carriage. One company can hold both, and many hold only one.

Whose customs bond should be used?

Either yours or the broker's, and it matters when you change brokers. Ask whose name the bond is in, what it covers and what happens to it if the relationship ends.
