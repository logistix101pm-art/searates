Ocean freight rate update coverage is dense with numbers and thin with method, and the freight market rewards the opposite. Freight rates get dated in news-shaped writing because a rate without a week is worthless, and the habit is worth borrowing. Analysis-shaped writing forecasts without saying what the forecast rests on, which is worth avoiding.

Three ocean freight pages of 74 do both — forecast and method in the same piece. Their approach sets the standard SeaRates holds itself to here.

Ocean freight rate update answers assembled by Google in this cluster cite the primary publisher more often than the commentary about it, which is unusual — in every other cluster measured on this project the vendor blogs lead. Of the ten domains cited most across the 59 AI answers captured on 07/26/2026, 4 carriers publishing their own advisories account for 21 citations, the customs regulator for 4 and the index publisher for 3 — 28 of the 46 citations those ten domains received between them.

The consequence for any ocean freight piece published here is direct. A piece that restates a carrier's surcharge advisory competes with the advisory itself, which the model is already citing and which will always be more current. What is not already cited is the count across publishers, the disagreement between two of them, and the arithmetic that turns an index level into a delivered cost.

When two sources disagree

Ocean freight rate update material inherits its sources' disagreements, and hiding them would be the easy option. Schedule reliability for mid-2026 is a live example: Sea-Intelligence puts May 2026 at 64.7% and calls it the best month of the year, while a Kuehne+Nagel seaexplorer summary gives June at 56.6% and calls April the best month at 62.4%. Both agree on April.

Freight rates and reliability figures are published here with both readings attributed, each period named, and the choice left to the reader — 5 of 74 pages measured explain their method well enough to make that possible. Picking one and presenting it as the figure would be quicker and would defeat the purpose of publishing at all.

Divergence of that size happens because publishers count different vessel sets and define on-time differently. A reader comparing carriers should check which publication a number came from before treating it as settled.

The two formats, and what each promises

Rate reports are numbered and scheduled; explainers are not. Both carry the same evidentiary standard, and they differ in what they claim.

  • Numbered reports. A recurring series with a fixed panel, published on a schedule. Each issue states the sample, the collection window and what changed since the previous number. Comparability between issues is the point, so the panel does not change silently.
  • Explainers. One-off pieces answering a question the search result answers badly. Each carries at least one measurement or primary source of its own; a piece that only restates what is already published does not run. In the corpus measured here, 13 of 74 pages cite an industry index and not one states the sample behind a figure of its own.

Ocean freight forecasts do not run in either format without the method behind them. Where SeaRates has no data, the piece says so — the phrase "not measured" appears across the project more often than any prediction does.

Regular rate reporting by median is not running yet, and the reason is honest: the quote array behind it does not exist. Until it does, rate material here cites indices with their dates rather than presenting a market average as our own.

Two ratios fall out of the ocean freight question pool, and neither of them flatters the plan. Of the 4,967 questions collected, 48.5% sit inside the plan as clustered articles, 33.6% clustered with nothing and wait in the tail file, and the remaining 17.8% were removed by the filter as noise or as already answered on a core page. The three shares account for the whole pool, which is the arithmetic a content plan should survive before a word of it is written.

The ocean freight plan is also more concentrated than eight hubs makes it sound: freight forwarding alone holds 42.4% of the planned articles and 39.9% of the questions behind them. The eight hub rows reconcile to 771 articles and 2,410 questions exactly rather than approximately, and across the whole plan an article answers 3.1 questions on average — the number that decides whether an article has enough to say or is a heading in search of a subject.

Ocean freight demand around this hub was read again on 07/28/2026 through the fan-out evidence map, and the result is a check on the plan rather than a gap in the page. Of 147 subqueries carrying a confidence of 0.6 or better, 99 are not answered by this hub at all — and 64 of those 99 are dated market events: a general rate increase announced for a month, a peak season surcharge on one trade, blank sailings, a canal restriction, a tariff action with a section number.

Ocean freight events of that shape do not belong on a hub, because a hub that answered them would be out of date the week after it was written. They belong to individual dated reports, which is what the 771-article plan above exists to carry. The largest non-event cluster left over is the customs bond — what the amount is, how it is calculated, how long one takes to obtain — and the plan already assigns that to the customs and duty hub.

Where each article sends the reader

Every ocean freight article here ends in the same three places, because a question about freight almost always resolves into three: what the number is, who would do the work, and whether that company is who it says it is.

Ocean freight questions routed that way — question, figure, provider, verification — make the blog useful rather than decorative, and 13 of the 74 pages measured cite any industry source at all. A reader who arrives at an explainer about detention should leave knowing how the demurrage and detention clock runs; one reading about quotes should leave with the cost of the container door to door; anyone about to book should leave with the licence and bond behind the company name.

Comparisons of providers live on their own pages: what the forwarder league tables measure for intermediaries and which ocean carrier actually runs your box for the lines themselves.

How often do ocean freight rates change?

Spot rates move weekly, which is why an index print carries its week: Drewry's WCI was $4,374 per 40-foot container in the assessment of 07/23/2026, down 4%. Contract rates move on their own cycle and do not track the index closely.

Does SeaRates forecast rates?

Not without a published method. Of the 74 pages measured in this search result, 29 forecast and 5 explain their method — SeaRates will not add to the first group without joining the second.

What is a GRI and when does it take effect?

A general rate increase is a carrier-announced uplift with a stated effective date; 27 of the 74 pages measured name such a date. The date matters more than the amount, because an increase announced and then withdrawn is common.

Why do two reports give different schedule reliability numbers?

Because publishers count different vessels and define on-time arrival differently. Sea-Intelligence put May 2026 at 64.7%; a Kuehne+Nagel summary gives June at 56.6% and April as the year's best at 62.4%. Both figures appear here with their sources.

Can I reprint a chart or figure from here?
