Ocean freight rate benchmarks for the Far East to US West Coast trade disagreed by $1,189 per 40ft container in mid-July 2026 — a three-way, 20.2% spread across Drewry, Xeneta and Freightos. Rechecked on the two benchmarks that publish without a paid account, that gap has closed to $413, or 5.6%:

Drewry's composite index, the container rate most often quoted without a lane attached, held stable at $4,476 per 40ft for a second consecutive week in the 09/10/2026 assessment. A composite averages many trades, so it understates this lane: on that date it sat 39% below the Shanghai to Los Angeles assessment — a wider gap than in July, because the transpacific lane rose sharply while several other trades in the composite did not.

Two different ocean freight rate readings on one trade are not a contradiction. Drewry assessed one port pair; Freightos measured a different basket, aggregated from live carrier and forwarder transactions rather than a single assessment. A freight rate benchmark is a definition before it is a number, and two definitions rarely agree exactly — though these two, this time, came close. What follows is what each one leaves out.

An index level is not a quote you can book

Container shipping rate indices publish the ocean leg and stop there, which is why an index level and an invoice rarely match. Of 196 pages ranking in US search across this lane cluster that SeaRates measured on 07/26/2026, 31 mention any public index at all and 2 warn the reader that the index level is not what they will be charged.

These charges sit outside every published index figure:

  • Terminal handling charges (THC) at origin and destination, billed per container by the terminal, not the carrier. Mentioned on 38 of the 196 pages measured.
  • Bunker adjustment factor (BAF), the fuel component, which moves independently of the base rate. Mentioned on 24 pages.
  • Peak season surcharge (PSS), applied by trade and withdrawn without notice. Mentioned on 28 pages.
  • General rate increase (GRI), a step change carriers announce for a whole trade. Mentioned on 13 pages.
  • Inland drayage and chassis, which turn a port-to-port container rate into a delivered one and are priced by distance, not by box.

Public indices are quoted per 40ft container, and no public index prices a 20ft box. Drewry's World Container Index, the Freightos Baltic Index and Xeneta's XSI all assess the 40-foot equivalent unit; across the 196 measured pages, none referenced an index tracking a 20ft container. A 20ft rate therefore always comes from a quote, never from a benchmark, and anyone showing you a "20ft index" has built it themselves.

Such thin index literacy makes one move practical: treat any published index as a direction of travel and take the level from a quote instead. The next section measures how far those quotes scatter.

20ft, 40ft and 40HC: what the rate is actually attached to

Ocean freight rates are quoted against a specific box, and three boxes dominate this trade. Quoting a rate without naming the equipment is the most common way two "identical" quotes end up thousands of dollars apart.

EquipmentExterior heightInternal volumeHow it is priced

A 40ft high cube stands one foot taller than a 40ft standard and adds roughly 344 cubic feet, about 9.7 CBM, for the same footprint on the vessel. Read 40HC vs 40ft standard as a volume question rather than a price one: high cube is the default box on the transpacific for light, bulky cargo, which is why so many quotes read "40HC" even when the enquiry said "40ft", and why the two are priced within a few dollars of each other rather than proportionally to volume.

A 20ft container is never half the price of a 40ft on the same sailing. Carriers sell slot space and handling, and a 20ft box takes one slot and one full set of terminal moves just as a 40ft does. Across the seven port pairs SeaRates captured on 07/25/2026, quoted 20ft container rates sat below the 40ft bands but nowhere near half — cargo weight and cube decide the box, not a wish to halve the shipping cost.

Equipment appears far less often in the published answers than price does: of the 196 pages measured on 07/26/2026, 44 mention high cube at all and 57 use TEU or FEU. Getting the box right is the first of the two things that decide a rate; the second is which coast the box lands on.

West Coast or East Coast: where cheaper freight stops being cheaper

Ocean freight rates from China to the US East Coast carried a $2,374 premium over the West Coast in Drewry's 09/10/2026 assessment — $9,726 per 40ft on Shanghai to New York against $7,352 on Shanghai to Los Angeles, a 32% difference. Freightos read the same shape at a higher level on 09/13/2026: $9,724 per FEU (FBX03) into the East Coast against $7,765 (FBX01) into the West Coast, a 25% gap. Both benchmarks moved up since July, and the East Coast premium widened on Drewry's reading (29% to 32%) while the two indices' own gap on the West Coast leg narrowed.

The East Coast ocean freight premium buys distance, and it is only worth paying when the inland leg would cost more. A box landed at Los Angeles for a Chicago or Dallas consignee still crosses the country by rail or truck, and drayage plus linehaul frequently exceeds the ocean premium. A box landed at Savannah for a South-East consignee saves that move entirely.

The ocean freight rate is not the only thing that changes with coast choice; the risks around it change too. West Coast routings put cargo through the San Pedro Bay complex, where congestion shows up as waiting time and demurrage rather than as freight rates. East Coast all-water routings depend on canal transit and longer schedules that leave more room for a missed connection. Transit deserves its own measurement rather than a single headline number. Terminal-level cost sits below the coast decision: what this port costs and how long boxes sit there prices the delay separately from the ocean rate above, and for a specific inland market such as Chicago, which port of entry serves this inland market runs the same comparison one destination at a time.

Port-to-port and door-to-door are different measurements, and conflating them is the commonest transit error on this trade. Port-to-port counts vessel departure to vessel arrival. What moved the rate since July

Container shipping rates moved again between July and September 2026, and the transpacific leg moved differently from the rest of Drewry's index. The composite WCI held stable at $4,476 per 40ft for a second consecutive week in the assessment dated 09/10/2026 — but Shanghai to Los Angeles rose 2% that same week to $7,352, and Shanghai to New York rose 1% to $9,726, meaning the trade this page covers firmed while the broader composite sat flat.

Forces pushing the transpacific rate up through early September, per Drewry's 09/10/2026 commentary:

  • Blank sailings. Eight sailings were cancelled for the following week, up from seven the week before — carriers actively withdrawing capacity to support the rate rather than responding to a demand spike.
  • Typhoon disruption. Freightos flagged typhoon-driven port congestion in its 09/02/2026 market update, adding delay and tightening effective capacity at Asian load ports.
  • A carrier surcharge revision on file. Ocean Network Express filed a revised Bunker Surcharge (OBS) tariff effective 10/01/2026 through 12/31/2026, applying globally across its FMC and non-FMC trades.
  • Iran–US tensions. Continued disruption through the Strait of Hormuz keeps a risk premium in the market, per Drewry's 09/10/2026 note, though it is not lane-specific to the transpacific.

Forces working the other way, mostly on Asia–Europe rather than this lane specifically: Shanghai port congestion eased from 94 hours in Week 35 to 64 hours in Week 36, and a selective return of services to the Suez Canal is easing Asia–Europe capacity — which is why Drewry's Shanghai–Genoa and Shanghai–Rotterdam readings fell the same week the transpacific rose. A composite index blending both trades can look flat even when its two largest components are moving in opposite directions.

What sits on the invoice above the ocean rate

Container shipping cost from China to USA reaches your accounts payable as a stack of line items, and the ocean freight rate is only the first of them. Comparing two quotes means putting both on the same basis before comparing any number.

These are the charges, in the order they usually appear:

  • Origin charges. Factory-to-port trucking of the container, export customs, documentation and origin terminal handling. Excluded from an FOB quote, included in an EXW one.
  • Ocean freight. The port-to-port number, the only part any index assesses.
  • Surcharges. BAF, PSS, GRI and any war-risk or emergency fuel component, each applied per container and each able to move between quote and sailing.
  • Destination terminal handling. Billed at the US port on arrival, independent of the carrier's freight rate.
  • US entry. Importer Security Filing, customs entry, a customs bond and brokerage. Duties are assessed on cargo value, not on freight.
  • Drayage and chassis. Port to warehouse, priced by distance and chassis availability rather than by box size.

Container shipping cost components are documented unevenly by the pages that rank on this lane. Of the 196 pages SeaRates measured on 07/26/2026, 60 mention an Incoterm or one of FOB, EXW, DDP and CIF, 32 mention the Importer Security Filing, 19 name both origin and destination charges, 16 mention drayage and 11 mention a customs bond at all.

Incoterms decide who pays which block, and a DDP quote bundles all of them into one figure that looks cheaper to read and harder to audit. Asking for the same shipment quoted both as a port-to-port rate plus a named list of surcharges and as an all-in DDP number is the fastest way to see what a forwarder has priced in. Dollar figures for each line item sit under the full landed cost breakdown, duty included, because they belong to a cost model rather than to a lane.

LCL and FCL: where the per-cubic-metre price stops making sense

Less than container load freight is billed on a revenue ton, not on a cubic metre, and the distinction decides whether an LCL quote is cheap or expensive. Under the weight-or-measure rule, a consolidator charges the greater of your volume in cubic metres and your weight in metric tons — 1,000 kg (2,205 lbs) counts as one revenue ton against 1 CBM (35.3 cubic feet). Dense cargo therefore pays on weight, and the headline per-CBM rate never applies to it.

Three more rules change the arithmetic:

  • A 1 CBM minimum. LCL consolidators bill a floor of one cubic metre even when the shipment measures less, so a 0.6 CBM consignment pays for 1 CBM.
  • Container freight station charges at both ends. Consolidation at origin and deconsolidation at destination are billed per CBM per side and are not part of the ocean quote.
  • A crossover point. The volume at which a 20ft FCL beats LCL is commonly placed near 13–15 CBM on this trade, and it moves: when FCL rates fall, the crossover falls with them.

SeaRates does not publish a per-CBM LCL rate for China to the United States, and the reason is specific rather than coy. Published per-CBM rates for this lane differ by roughly a factor of two between sources — including between two articles by the same publisher — and a number that unstable would fail a consensus check on any given week. Ask for the LCL quote as a total for your measured cargo, with the CFS charges itemised, and the per-CBM headline stops mattering.

That 13–15 CBM crossover decides the FCL-or-LCL question on the China lane; on other trades the question is whether a benchmark exists at all.

Book now or wait: what actually decides it

Ocean freight booking timing is a capacity decision before it is a price decision. Nobody publishing a container rate page — including this one — knows where the spot market goes next month, and a page telling you to book now because rates are rising is describing the past.

Conditions that argue for booking early rather than waiting:

  • A fixed cargo ready date inside the August-to-October window. Peak season demand tightens space, and a container booking made 4 to 6 weeks ahead is materially less likely to be rolled than one made a week out.
  • Cargo that cannot be split. A single large consignment has fewer fallback options when a sailing is blanked.
  • A destination that depends on a specific inland connection. Missing a rail cut-off costs more days than missing a vessel.

Conditions that argue for flexibility:

  • A slack-season ready date, conventionally March through June, when carriers compete for container volume and space is easier to find late.
  • Cargo that can wait a sailing. Flexibility on the ready date is the only real negotiating lever a small shipper holds.
  • Annual volume large enough for a contract. Contract rates trade upside for stability, which is worth more to a business that cannot reprice its own goods weekly.

Ocean freight booking advice in this market is lopsided, which is why so much of it is unusable. Of the 196 pages SeaRates measured on 07/26/2026, 76 mention peak season, 4 mention the slack season it is measured against, 16 give a booking lead time in weeks and 23 name the cargo ready date that lead time is counted from.

Locking an ocean freight rate is not the same as locking capacity. A quote with no allocation behind it can still be rolled, and the question to ask a forwarder is whether space is confirmed on a named vessel, not whether the price holds. Before agreeing to any container rate, take two minutes to check whether the company holds an FMC OTI licence: an unlicensed intermediary is a separate problem from an expensive one.

Why your container is later than the transit you were quoted

Schedule reliability across global container shipping was 56.6% in June 2026 — fewer than three sailings in five arrived within their scheduled window — down 3.7 points on May and 2.3 points on June 2025, according to Sea-Intelligence data published through Kuehne+Nagel. April 2026, the best month of the year, reached 62.4%, and vessels late that month arrived an average of 5.34 days behind schedule. Those figures are global rather than lane-specific: a per-lane, per-carrier breakdown sits behind paid datasets, and SeaRates does not publish one.

Failure modes that turn a quoted transit into a longer one:

  • Rolled cargo. The carrier overbooked the vessel or hit a weight limit, and your container waits for the next sailing, typically a week. Mentioned on 25 of the 196 pages SeaRates measured on 07/26/2026.
  • Blank sailings. A scheduled departure is cancelled to manage capacity, and every booking on it moves. Mentioned on 20 pages.
  • Transshipment. A routing that changes vessel at an intermediate hub adds a connection that can be missed. Mentioned on 40 pages.
  • Customs examination. A document query or physical inspection holds the container after discharge, adding days no carrier schedule accounts for.
  • Chassis and drayage. A released container still needs a chassis and a driver, and neither is certain to be free on the day.

Asking which of these failures hit your container is more useful than asking for a new estimated arrival. Rolled cargo and blank sailings are visible to the carrier immediately; customs and equipment problems are visible to your broker and your drayage provider. Delay costs money in a specific way that has its own clock, and the clock starts at the terminal gate.

Free time starts before you notice it

Demurrage and detention are two different container charges separated by the terminal fence, and confusing them is the most expensive vocabulary error in importing. Demurrage accrues while your container sits inside the terminal past its free days. Detention accrues once you have taken the carrier's container out and are holding it past the allowance, whether it is at your dock, in a yard or waiting to be returned.

Free time is quoted in days from discharge, and the counter runs on calendar days at most US terminals whether or not you have been notified. Of the 196 pages SeaRates measured on 07/26/2026, 25 mention demurrage, 19 mention detention, and 5 explain the difference. Daily rates, free-time allowances and the billing rules that let a shipper dispute an invoice sit under how the demurrage and detention clock runs, because they belong to the charge rather than to the lane.

How to sanity-check a quote in five minutes

Ocean freight quotes become comparable once five things are pinned down, and pinning them down takes less time than collecting a third quote. Work through them in order:

1) Fix the unit. Confirm the container rate is per 40ft or per 40HC, and get the 20ft price separately if you need it. 2) Fix the date and the validity. A rate needs the day it was assessed and the day it expires. Spot quotes on this trade commonly hold for days, not months. 3) Fix the basis. Port-to-port, port-to-door or door-to-door, and which Incoterm the origin charges sit under. 4) List what is excluded. Ask for surcharges by name — BAF, PSS, GRI, war risk — rather than "plus local charges", and get destination THC in writing. 5) Measure the spread to a benchmark. Compare the ocean component against the current Drewry, Freightos or Xeneta reading, and treat a quote far below every benchmark as a question rather than a bargain.

Quote hygiene is measurable, and the pages ranking on this lane do not have much of it. Of the 196 pages SeaRates measured on 07/26/2026, 83 state whether a figure is port-to-port or door-to-door, 53 mention a validity or an expiry, 24 ask the reader to have charges itemised, and 12 suggest comparing a quote against a published benchmark.

A quote that survives all five is comparable to another quote that survives all five, and nothing else is. Comparing forwarders themselves, rather than their shipping costs, is a separate exercise covered in the forwarder scorecard and how it is built.

Nobody publishing container rate pages knows where spot goes next month. Book early when the ready date is fixed inside peak season or the consignment cannot be split; stay flexible when it can move.

Does the US East Coast always cost more than the West Coast?

On the 09/10/2026 Drewry assessment the East Coast carried a 32% premium per 40ft (up from 29% in July). Total cost depends on the inland leg: a Savannah discharge for a South-East consignee can be cheaper delivered.

What is the difference between demurrage and detention?

Demurrage accrues while the container sits inside the terminal past its free days. Detention accrues after you take the carrier's container out and hold it past the allowance. Only 5 of 196 ranking pages measured explain the distinction.

What should a complete container rate quote list?

A complete quote names the equipment, the assessment date, the validity, the basis and every surcharge. Of 196 ranking pages measured on 07/26/2026, none stated the sample behind the rate they published.
