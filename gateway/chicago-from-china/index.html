Which US port should your Chicago container come through?

By SeaRates · Updated 13 September 2026 · Every figure carries its unit, its reading date and a linked source · Send a correction

⚑ Where the money actually goes

Import gateways are decided on the landside bill rather than on the ocean rate: the sea leg differs by days, the inland leg differs by dollars, and only the second is fully under your control. Price both legs to the warehouse door instead of to the berth — of 95 pages ranking on this question, three put a dollar figure on both.

The gateway decision is a landside decision, and the published answers do not price it

Gateway ports are argued about on the ocean rate and paid for on the 4 landside charges that follow discharge. SeaRates measured the 95 pages that Google United States ranks or cites across the 25 queries in this cluster, drawn from 72 domains and read on 07/26/2026:

  • 57 of 95 pages (60.0%) name two or more US gateway ports.
  • 50 of 95 (52.6%) name two or more gateways next to a named inland market such as Chicago, Dallas, Atlanta, Memphis or Denver.
  • 11 of 95 (11.6%) put a dollar figure anywhere near drayage, intermodal rail, linehaul or trucking.
  • 3 of 95 (3.2%) put a dollar figure on the ocean leg and the inland leg in the same document.

Gateway recommendations from Google's own answers are narrower still. Across the 24 AI Overviews the same 25 queries produced on 07/25 and 07/26/2026, SeaRates counted 21 that mention rail or intermodal movement, 4 that mention drayage, and none at all — 0 of 24 — that mention a chassis, demurrage or detention. Import gateways are being recommended by a machine that never costs the wheels under the box.

Inland gateway arithmetic has five moving parts, and the ocean rate is one of them:

  1. Ocean freight to the discharge port, quoted per 40ft box and owned by the trade lane, not by the warehouse.
  2. Terminal handling and release at the gateway, billed by the terminal on its own schedule.
  3. Drayage at the gateway, the short truck move from marine terminal to rail ramp or transload dock.
  4. Rail linehaul plus fuel to the destination ramp, which is where distance stops behaving predictably.
  5. Destination drayage, chassis and free time, the part that turns a delivered container into an invoice nobody forecast.

Import gateway comparisons that stop at line one are unfinished rather than wrong. The rest of this page prices lines two to five one market at a time, starting with the market behind 12 of the 25 queries in this cluster.

When the West Coast wins for a Midwest warehouse, and when the East Coast does

West Coast gateway ports win a Midwest warehouse whenever the East Coast ocean premium, put at 30% to 65% for Asian cargo by Google's answer of 07/25/2026, is larger than the gap between the two inland legs. Dbhorsestable.com quotes the same 30-65% band. SeaRates publishes no ocean rate level on this page, because a level belongs with its benchmark and its date on what a container from China really costs.

Land bridge economics are frequently quoted at the wrong length. The most cited breakdown in the measured set prices a rail leg of $1,500 to $2,500 per 40ft container — local drayage $200 to $400, main rail run $1,000 to $1,800, terminal handling $300 to $500 — but that measurement runs from a West Coast port all the way to the East Coast, roughly 900 rail miles further than Chicago. Copying the figure onto a Chicago quote overstates the inland leg by about a third.

Coastal gateway choice shifts with the calendar rather than staying fixed. Congestion appears on 31 of the 95 measured pages and terminal dwell on 11 — enough to establish that both coasts queue, not enough to say which one is queuing this month. Reviewing the decision quarterly costs nothing. Before any of that arithmetic matters, importers have to stop confusing two different things that Google calls a port of entry.

A CBP port of entry is a code, not a berth

Port of entry in the CBP sense is an administrative office with a 4-digit code, and it does not have to sit on water at all. Not one of the 95 pages SeaRates measured on 07/26/2026 separates the CBP port of entry from the port where the container is physically discharged; 7 of 95 name a port code at all, 2 mention the entry summary the code is written on, and 3 mention an in-bond move. Google's AI answer for Midwest imports, captured 07/26/2026, answers the question with Chicago (port code 3901), Detroit (3801) and Cleveland — inland customs offices, none of which can berth a container ship.

CBP publishes the lists that settle the point, and the addresses give it away:

Port of entry 1704 is an office beside Atlanta's airport, about 250 miles from the Savannah terminal where Atlanta's ocean containers actually land. Michigan alone carries eight CBP ports under the Detroit field office, including an airport at Lansing and a bridge plaza at Sault Sainte Marie. CBP's index page for locating a port of entry was last modified 09/12/2025 and is the source of record for every code above.

Entry filing and gateway routing are two decisions with two different owners. Customs entry can be filed at the gateway where the vessel discharges, or the container can travel under an in-bond move to an inland port of entry and clear there. Gateway selection changes drayage, rail and free time; port-of-entry selection changes where a broker files and where an examination happens. Duty rates change with neither — the tariff follows the goods, not the door. Timing changes with both, which is the next comparison.

Transit ranges of that width between gateway ports are a measurement problem before they are a service problem. Port-to-port counts vessel departure to vessel arrival and nothing else; door-to-door adds export clearance, terminal cut-off, discharge, customs release, the rail leg and the final truck. Of the 95 pages SeaRates measured on 07/26/2026, 25.3% state which of the two bases their number uses and 10.5% give the rail leg a day count at all. Published West Coast ranges span 14 days end to end against 19 days for the East Coast.

Intermodal rail days are the cheapest days in the chain to plan around. A West Coast gateway plus 3 to 5 rail days into Chicago lands inside roughly 20 to 25 days door to door according to iContainers, against the 25 to 35 days an all-water East Coast routing needs before the box has moved an inland mile. An East Coast routing buys the removal of a leg, not speed. Intermodal ramps in the Chicago district sit up to 50 road miles apart, and the drayage carrier prices every one of those miles. Mark-It Express, a Chicago drayage operator in the measured set, publishes a service radius of roughly 300 miles from Chicago and states interchange agreements at every major intermodal terminal in the district — which is the practical test of a drayage quote, because a carrier without an interchange agreement at your ramp cannot collect your box at all.

Destination ramps are assigned at booking, and importers can ask. A container routed to a ramp on the wrong side of the metropolitan area adds an hour of driver time in each direction plus tolls, and none of that cost appears in an ocean quote. Ramp-level dwell figures would settle which of the four is fastest, but no public dataset publishes dwell by destination ramp and SeaRates has none to offer. Free time at the ramp, by contrast, is knowable in advance, and almost nobody checks it.

Free time is where a cheap gateway becomes an expensive one

Free time at a gateway terminal is normally 3 to 5 days, and the clock does not care which port you chose. Demurrage accrues while the container sits inside the terminal past its free days; detention accrues once the carrier's box has left the terminal and is being held outside it. Of the 95 pages SeaRates measured on 07/26/2026, 14 mention demurrage, 13 mention detention, 9 mention both, exactly 1 explains the difference, and none states free time as a number of days.

Accessorial charges have a shape even when the level is stale, and one published drayage tariff in the measured set shows the shape. Kansas City Intermodal's rate sheet, dated on its own page 01/26/2023, lists:

Chassis charges appear on 11 of 95 measured pages and on none of the 24 AI answers, which makes them the most reliably invisible line in the gateway decision. Import gateways with cheap ocean rates and tight free time can cost more delivered than expensive gateways with generous free time, and the difference is decided in days rather than in dollars per container. Under the Federal Maritime Commission's final rule effective 05/28/2024, a demurrage or detention invoice must be issued within 30 calendar days of the charge being incurred. Rates, allowances and the grounds for disputing an invoice sit under how the demurrage and detention clock runs, because they belong to the charge rather than to the routing.

Inland ports: what Georgia built, and what an inland port cannot do

Inland ports are rail-served container yards that move terminal functions inland, and Georgia Ports Authority operates the 2 clearest US examples. Both facilities exist to take a truck round trip off the road between a deepwater gateway and a manufacturing region:

Inland ports: what Georgia built, and what an inland port cannot do

Appalachian Regional Port, Crandall, GA201842 acresCSX, 388 rail miles~50,000 containers a year

Inland port economics are stated in truck miles rather than in dollars per container by everyone who publishes them. FreightWaves reported on 05/06/2026 that the Gainesville facility is projected to take about 26,000 truckloads off Georgia highways and remove a 600-mile round trip for shippers in the north-east of the state; the Appalachian Regional Port is credited by its operators with roughly 15 million truck miles saved. Neither figure is a price, and neither answers what an individual importer pays.

Inland ports cannot do three things importers routinely expect of them. An inland port does not shorten the ocean leg, because the vessel still discharges at the deepwater gateway. An inland port relocates the drayage move rather than removing it — the truck runs at the inland yard instead of at the coast. An inland port does not extend free time, because the free-time clock belongs to the ocean carrier's tariff and not to the yard. Inland ports win on volume in one direction: enough containers to one region to fill regular service, which is the condition Georgia's two facilities were built around.

Atlanta and Memphis: the $1,000 claim, and what the study actually measured

Port of Savannah is the answer Google gives for both Atlanta and Memphis, and a study published 04/09/2026 puts a named source behind the claim. Researchers at the Georgia Tech Supply Chain and Logistics Institute found that shippers can save more than $1,000 per container by routing through Savannah rather than West Coast ports for cargo destined for Atlanta, Memphis and Nashville, once full end-to-end supply chain costs and transit reliability are both counted.

Gateway savings of that size come with boundaries worth stating out loud, because the study does not claim what the headline suggests:

  • Three named markets only. Atlanta, Memphis and Nashville — the finding is not a general statement about the Savannah gateway against West Coast ports for every inland market, and Chicago is not among the three.
  • End-to-end costs, not ocean rates. The comparison counts the whole chain including reliability, which is precisely the landside arithmetic 92 of the 95 measured pages do not perform.
  • A point in time. A study published 04/09/2026 describes the market it measured; ocean rate moves of 30% in either direction would change the answer without anyone rerunning it.

Published route bands for Atlanta show the shape of the eastern gateway option. Forest Leopard's table, read 07/25/2026, prices Shanghai via Savannah with drayage to Atlanta at $1,250 for a 20ft box and $2,550–$2,650 for a 40ft in 30 to 35 days, Ningbo via Savannah at $1,100 and $2,400–$2,500 in 30 to 36 days, and Qingdao via Charleston at $1,200 and $2,650–$2,750 in 33 to 40 days. Savannah sits 250 miles from Atlanta by road and Brunswick 300 miles, according to the Metro Atlanta Chamber, which puts the inland leg inside a single day's drive and explains the whole advantage in one number. Texas has no equivalent single answer, because two different kinds of gateway compete there.

Texas and Dallas: the Gulf, the West Coast, and why Laredo is a different question

Gulf Coast gateways and land border crossings are both called Texas ports, and only 1 of the 25 queries in this cluster gets the distinction back from Google. The AI answer for Texas imports, captured 07/25/2026, splits its own reply into overland trade through Port Laredo and maritime container trade through Port Houston. Laredo handles trucks and rail crossing from Mexico; Houston handles container vessels. A Dallas importer loading in China chooses among ocean gateways, and Laredo is not one of them.

Ocean gateway options for a Dallas warehouse come down to two, and each buys something different:

  • Los Angeles or Long Beach plus rail. Shortest ocean leg from North Asia, then an intermodal move into the DFW ramps. Published transpacific ocean transit of 13 to 27 days plus a rail leg, against a longer all-water alternative.
  • Houston, all water via the Panama Canal. Ocean transit runs 25 to 44 days in the published ranges, then a truck move of about 240 miles to Dallas — short enough that the rail discount never applies and long enough to need a full day's drayage.

Gulf gateway routings trade weeks of ocean time for the removal of an intermodal rail leg, exactly as East Coast ports do, with one extra exposure. Hurricane season concentrates weather risk on a single stretch of coast between June and November, and a Dallas importer running everything through one Gulf gateway has no second door. Splitting volume across Houston and a West Coast gateway costs a little in rate tiers and buys a working alternative in the season a Gulf gateway is most likely to close. The canal that makes the Houston routing possible is the constraint most often described incorrectly.

The Panama Canal limits the schedule, not the map

Panama Canal restrictions change how many ships reach an East Coast or Gulf gateway port and how deeply they load, adding 2 to 5 days to a transit rather than closing a route. SeaRates counted the canal on 13 of 95 measured pages, the word draft in any sense on 5, an actual draft or water-level restriction as a routing factor on 3, and a canal surcharge on 3; among the 24 AI answers, exactly 1 mentions draft at all. Import gateway pages are recommending all-water routings while almost never naming the physical limit those routings run through.

Draft restrictions on a gateway routing work through three effects, and each lands on a different line of an importer's cost:

  1. Fewer daily transits. ExFreight, read 07/25/2026, is the source of the 2 to 5 extra days a vessel spends reaching a gateway port, and attributes the cap on daily transits to drought restrictions on the waterway.
  2. Less cargo per vessel. A draft limit caps how deeply a ship may load, so carriers either leave boxes behind or redistribute weight, and the cost of the shortfall arrives as a surcharge.
  3. A schedule that stops being weekly. dbhorsestable.com notes that conditions improved after the severe drought of 2023-2024 while capacity remains limited, which produces delays and surcharges rather than cancellations.

Maximum authorised draft for the locks, the number deciding which gateway ports a fully loaded vessel can serve, is published by the Panama Canal Authority as an advisory and changes with rainfall, so SeaRates prints no figure for it here: a lock restriction is worth nothing without the date it was authorised, and the canal authority's advisories are the only source of record. Receiving terminals impose the mirror-image limit at the other end — the Port of New York and New Jersey publishes 50 feet of shipping channel and 215 feet of air draft on its own site. Any gateway comparison that treats an all-water routing as a fixed transit time has assumed away both limits.

Two legs, two regulators: FMC on the water, FMCSA on the road

Gateway routings cross 2 regulatory regimes, and no single document covers both. An ocean transportation intermediary arranging the sea leg needs an FMC licence; a motor carrier performing the drayage needs FMCSA operating authority, a USDOT number and, for interchanging ocean containers, a UIIA agreement. FMC appears on 3 of the 95 pages SeaRates measured and FMCSA on 2 — in a cluster whose entire subject is the seam between the two legs.

USDOT and MC numbers say nothing at all about the ocean leg into a gateway port. A company can display genuine FMCSA authority for trucking it really performs and hold no licence whatever to organise the sea movement it is quoting. No single document covers an international door-to-door move end to end, so gateway due diligence is three checks:

  1. Ocean leg. Confirm the FMC licence of whoever issues the bill of lading for the gateway routing, and confirm it covers the role they are playing rather than a related one.
  2. Inland leg. Confirm FMCSA authority and a live UIIA registration for the drayage carrier at your destination ramp, because interchange rights are ramp-specific.
  3. Customs. Confirm the broker is licensed by CBP, a separate examination unrelated to either of the above.

Verification takes minutes before a rate is agreed and days after a container is stuck. Our guide to check whether the company holds an FMC OTI licence covers the registry lookups for the ocean leg.

What a comparable gateway quote has to list

Gateway quotes become comparable only when 6 things are pinned down, and pinning them down takes less time than collecting a third quote. Work through them in order:

  1. Name the destination. Ramp delivery and store-door delivery are different products; a ramp price against a door price is not a comparison. Only 24 of 95 measured pages state which basis their figure uses.
  2. Name the gateway and the ramp. Long Beach to Bedford Park and Long Beach to Joliet are different final drays, and 13 of 95 pages name a ramp at all.
  3. Split the legs. Ask for ocean, terminal, origin dray, rail linehaul, fuel and destination dray as separate lines. Requests to itemise appear on 3 of 95 pages.
  4. Date the quote and get its validity. A date beside the price appears on 10 of 95 measured pages, and a validity or expiry on 4.
  5. Ask for free time in days at both ends. No page in the measured set — 0 of 95 — states free time as a number of days, so the number will only ever come from asking.
  6. List the accessorials by name. Chassis, chassis split, pre-pull, detention per hour, storage at the ramp. Vague "plus local charges" is where the difference between two gateways disappears.

Quote hygiene across gateway ports is thin enough to measure. None of the 95 pages SeaRates read on 07/26/2026 report a median, a percentile or the sample behind their figure, and 7 use an open-ended "from $X" with no upper bound. A price with no sample and no date is a claim rather than a measurement, which applies to this page too — every figure above carries its source and the day it was read. Duty, government fees and destination charges sit under the full landed cost breakdown, duty included.

What goes wrong after the ship docks, and how to test a gateway before it does

Gateway problems almost all appear after discharge, and global schedule reliability of 56.6% in June 2026 — down 3.7 points on May — makes a late arrival the normal case rather than the exception. Each failure is visible to a different party first:

  • Rolled cargo and blanked sailings. The booking at the gateway port moves to a later vessel and every downstream date moves with it, and the carrier sees it first. Only 1 of the 95 pages SeaRates measured mentions either failure.
  • A customs examination. The container is held after discharge for a document query or an inspection, and free time keeps running. Examinations and holds appear on 21 of 95 pages, more than any other failure mode.
  • No chassis at the ramp. A released box still needs wheels and a driver; chassis appear on 11 of 95 pages and on none of the 24 AI answers.
  • Terminal or ramp congestion. Congestion appears on 31 of 95 measured pages and dwell on 11, which is the closest the public record gets to naming a slow gateway.
  • Storage at the destination ramp. Ramp storage charges appear on 0 of 95 pages, which does not mean they are rare — it means nobody writes about them until they are billed.

Gateway failure modes decide routings more often than rates do, because one bad month at a congested gateway costs more than a year of a $200 rate difference. Testing a different gateway before that month arrives takes four parameters: five to ten containers over one quarter; one measurement per container, being total delivered cost to the dock plus the discharge and unload dates; one control set moving through the incumbent gateway in the same weeks, because a comparison run in different months compares seasons rather than ports; and one decision rule written down before the data arrives.

Pilot results will not show what a congested peak season looks like at the new gateway, and no pilot shorter than a year will. Gateway pilots measure cost and normal-condition transit; resilience is bought by holding a second gateway open, which is a different decision at a different price. Ten containers of your own invoices will still beat every published band on this page, including the ones SeaRates measured — your own cargo is a better sample than somebody else's market.

Frequently asked questions

Chicago is CBP port 3901, an inland customs port, not a container seaport. Port 3901 is the CBP port code for Chicago, Illinois. CBP publishes a state-by-state directory under "Locate a Port of Entry", last modified 09/12/2025, listing every port name, code and address.

What port of entry is in Chicago?

Chicago is CBP port 3901, an inland customs port, not a container seaport. Ocean containers destined for Chicago are discharged at a coastal gateway and moved inland by rail; the CBP code identifies where an entry is filed, not where the ship berths.

What is port 3901?

Port 3901 is the CBP port code for Chicago, Illinois. A port code identifies an administrative CBP office on the entry summary. Codes exist for airports, land crossings and inland offices as well as seaports.

How do I find out my port of entry?

Your customs broker files the entry at a specific CBP port, and the code appears on your entry summary. CBP publishes a state-by-state directory under "Locate a Port of Entry", last modified 09/12/2025, listing every port name, code and address.

Can container ships get to Chicago?

Ocean container ships cannot reach Chicago. Great Lakes access runs through the St. Lawrence Seaway, whose locks are far too small for a modern container vessel, so every ocean container billed to Chicago arrives by rail or truck from a coastal gateway.

What is the closest port to Dallas?

Port Houston is the closest deepwater container gateway to Dallas, roughly 240 miles south. Most transpacific cargo for Dallas still arrives through Los Angeles or Long Beach and moves by intermodal rail, because the ocean leg is 10 to 20 days shorter.

What is the closest port to Tennessee?

Port of Savannah is the container gateway most often used for Memphis, and Georgia Tech research published 04/09/2026 found it saves more than $1,000 per container against West Coast routings for Memphis cargo. The International Port of Memphis handles river barge traffic, not ocean containers.

What is an inland port?

An inland port is a rail-served container yard that moves terminal functions inland from a deepwater gateway. Gainesville Inland Port in Georgia, opened 05/04/2026, cost $134 million on 104 acres and connects to Savannah over Norfolk Southern rail.

What is the main difference between a seaport and an inland port?

A seaport berths vessels; an inland port never sees a ship. An inland port relocates the drayage move rather than removing it, does not shorten the ocean leg, and does not extend the ocean carrier's free time, which belongs to the carrier's tariff.

What are Georgia's two deepwater ports?

Georgia's deepwater ports are Savannah and Brunswick. Savannah handles containers and sits 250 miles from Atlanta; Brunswick, 300 miles from Atlanta, is a major vehicle port. The state's inland ports at Gainesville and Crandall feed Savannah by rail.

Is there a port in Atlanta, Georgia?

Atlanta has a CBP port of entry — code 1704 at 157 Tradeport Drive — but no water port. Ocean containers for Atlanta discharge at Savannah or Charleston and travel the last 250 miles or so by truck or rail.

Is it cheaper to ship by rail or truck?

Intermodal rail is cheaper than truckload on long lanes and loses the advantage on short ones. InTek Logistics puts the intermodal advantage at 10-15% on lanes over 700 miles, with drayage eroding it below 500 miles; their Los Angeles to Chicago example runs $1,700 intermodal against $3,500 truckload.

What is CSX intermodal?

CSX Intermodal is the container and trailer division of CSX, an eastern US Class I railroad. CSX operates the Bedford Park ramp in the Chicago district and the 388-mile rail link that serves Georgia's Appalachian Regional Port.

Which gateway port is cheapest for a Denver warehouse?

Denver cargo from Asia routes through a West Coast gateway in every published answer SeaRates read, with total transit of 25 to 35 days. Denver sits outside the dense intermodal corridors, so the final leg is frequently a truck move rather than a ramp pull, and it deserves its own quote line.

How long does shipping take from China to Chicago?

Published door-to-door estimates run about 20 to 25 days through a West Coast gateway, made up of 13 to 21 ocean days plus 3 to 5 rail days, per iContainers read 07/25/2026. All-water East Coast routings run 25 to 35 ocean days before any inland move.
