US container ports are not separated by the ocean rate, because the ocean rate is much the same whichever gate the box leaves through. What a US container port charges you, and who sends each bill

A US container port charge is never one bill from one party: five different organizations invoice a single import container, and only two of them answer to the port authority. Of the 183 pages ranking in US search that SeaRates measured on 07/26/2026, 9 mention a terminal handling charge at all and 5 print a dollar figure beside it.

Terminal handling charge belongs to the ocean carrier's tariff rather than to the port's, and that single fact breaks most published port comparisons. Hapag-Lloyd's North America terminal handling schedule prices Baltimore at USD 600 for a 20-foot dry container and USD 750 for a 40-foot dry container. Seaboard Marine raised its US terminal handling charge on a 40-foot dry container from $250 to $325 effective 10/12/2025. A vendor comparison dated 05/23/2026 puts Savannah at roughly $290 to $370 per TEU, with wharfage of $60 to $80 per TEU.

Port authorities publish a separate document, and it covers the vessel more closely than it covers your box. The Port Authority of New York and New Jersey issues Marine Terminal Tariff FMC Schedule No. PA-10, which sets dockage, wharfage and demurrage for its public berths. Georgia Ports Authority publishes numbered rules of the same kind, including a minimum crane lift charge of $742.28 per lift at Savannah and Brunswick. Marine terminal operators file their own tariffs with the Federal Maritime Commission, which maintains the list of who has filed what.

No tariff anywhere publishes a column called "THC by US port", and a table that shows one has quietly chosen a carrier and a date without naming either. Ask for the carrier and the tariff date next to every terminal figure you are quoted, because without both the number cannot be compared with anything. Which US port is cheapest to import through

Which US port is cheapest to import through is not a fixed place but the gateway where terminal handling, drayage, free time and inland transit add up to the least for one specific destination. Google's own AI Overview for that query, captured on 07/25/2026, opens with the same qualifier: not a single fixed location, but whichever gateway minimizes total landed cost.

Four conditions decide it, and 3 of the 4 are settled on land:

  1. Where the goods are consumed. A West Coast entry through Los Angeles, Long Beach, Oakland or the Northwest Seaport Alliance carries the shortest ocean leg from Asia and the longest inland leg to the Atlantic seaboard. An East Coast entry through New York and New Jersey, Savannah, Charleston or Norfolk reverses both.
  2. How the box leaves the terminal. Rail-destined containers and truck-destined containers sit for different lengths of time at the same port, which is why one gateway can be fast for a drayman and slow for an intermodal shipper.
  3. How much free time the carrier grants, and when the clock starts. Free time in US import trades typically runs 3 to 7 days, and the start hour is set by the carrier's or terminal's tariff, not by the port.
  4. What the drayman charges to reach the warehouse. Local drayage out of Los Angeles and Long Beach ran roughly $400 to $900 per container in 2026 by the vendor figures Google cited on 07/25/2026, before a fuel surcharge of 15% to 20%.

Ocean freight rate is the wrong axis for a port decision, and 16 of the 183 pages SeaRates measured on 07/26/2026 use it anyway. Carriers price a trade lane and a network rather than a terminal, so the ocean line on two quotes into two competing US gateways moves together and rarely explains a difference in the final invoice. The lines that do explain it sit underneath: terminal handling, storage, chassis and the truck.

A cheap port for a Los Angeles distribution centre is an expensive port for a Charlotte one, and no ranking answers for both. Work the comparison per destination and per box size, using the same date on every figure. What the ocean leg itself costs into the United States belongs to the trade lane rather than to the port, and what a container from China really costs carries those numbers for the busiest one.

Container dwell time is four different numbers, and ports publish the flattering one

Container dwell time at a US port is 4 separate measurements wearing one name, and sources that quote a single figure have usually picked whichever is smallest. Of the 183 pages SeaRates measured on 07/26/2026, 38 mention dwell at all, 21 attach a number of days or hours to it, and 18 say which dwell they measured.

  • Import dwell counts the days a loaded import box sits on the terminal before it goes out the truck gate.
  • Rail dwell counts the wait for an on-dock or near-dock train, and it moves independently of the truck gate.
  • Vessel dwell counts the ship at berth, which matters to the carrier and almost never to the cargo owner.
  • Yard dwell counts everything standing in the container yard, including empties and export boxes, and runs higher than import dwell by construction.

Terminal free time counted from discharge and free time counted from availability are two different allowances, and the difference is worth a full day at the front of the window. A container discharged at 4:00 PM under the Georgia Ports Authority rule starts burning free time at 12:01 AM the same night, roughly eight hours before it would under a carrier tariff that starts at 08:00 on the first business day. Standard imports at Savannah are generally granted 4 to 5 days on that clock.

Free time from the carrier and free time from the terminal also run separately and can expire on different dates for one box. Carrier free time governs demurrage on the ocean carrier's invoice; terminal free time governs storage billed by the marine terminal operator. Asking a forwarder for "the last free day" without asking whose produces one date where there are two.

Drayage is the line that actually separates two US container ports

Drayage is the short-haul truck move from the terminal to a warehouse or rail ramp, and it is the largest single line that changes when you change port. Local drayage from the Ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach ran roughly $400 to $900 per container in 2026 according to the vendor sources Google's AI Overview cited on 07/25/2026, with a fuel surcharge normally quoted at 15% to 20% on top — which adds $60 to $180 to that range by direct calculation.

A drayage quote is a stack, and the base rate is the smallest part of the argument:

  • Base dray rate covers the terminal-to-door or terminal-to-ramp move, priced by distance band.
  • Fuel surcharge floats against a national fuel index and is quoted as a percentage of the base.
  • Chassis rental is charged per day, and a chassis split — collecting the chassis from a different location than the container — adds a second move.
  • Pre-pull moves the box off the terminal before the last free day and stores it on the trucker's yard, trading demurrage for storage.
  • Dry run and waiting time are billed when the container is not ready at the gate, and neither appears on the original quote.
  • Clean Truck Fund rate at the Port of Los Angeles is $10 per TEU for non-exempt trucks, part of the port's zero-emission transition targeted at 2035.

Comparable drayage rates across US ports do not exist in public form, and no honest page pretends otherwise. Drayage directories publish supply rather than price — 382 draymen are listed as serving the Savannah intermodal region — while rate histories are published per terminal and per destination ZIP rather than per port. Of the 183 pages SeaRates measured on 07/26/2026, 29 mention drayage and 5 put a dollar figure beside it; chassis appear on 25 and per diem on 3.

Quote drayage at each port the way you quote ocean freight: same destination ZIP, same box size, same week, all accessorials listed. Two ports separated by $300 of ocean rate can be separated by twice that on the truck, which is why SeaRates records the terminal and the accessorial list beside every drayage figure it publishes.

East Coast, West Coast or Gulf Coast: where the answer flips

The coast decision flips at the point where the extra ocean days of an East Coast port call cost less than the extra inland miles of a West Coast one, and that point moves with fuel, rail capacity and the shape of your order book rather than with the ocean rate. West Coast ports carry the shortest transit from Asia, commonly quoted at 14 to 28 days, and the longest land leg to the Atlantic seaboard.

Three variables move the crossover, and none of them is the freight rate:

  • Inland distance and mode. A rail move from San Pedro Bay to the Midwest competes with an all-water service to Savannah or New York and New Jersey; the winner changes when rail dwell rises, as it did at Los Angeles and Long Beach in April 2026, when rail-destined containers averaged 5.06 days against 4.41 in March.
  • Cargo urgency. Time-sensitive cargo pays for the shortest ocean leg regardless of the inland arithmetic, and seasonal goods with a hard sell-by date behave the same way.
  • Volume shape. Continuous flow rewards the gateway with the deepest sailing schedule; sporadic single containers reward the gateway closest to the door, because a single box cannot amortise a rail ramp.

Gulf Coast ports serve a different cargo profile again. Port Houston ranks first among US ports in foreign waterborne tonnage at 220.1 million short tons in 2024, dominated by energy and petrochemicals, with a growing container franchise serving Texas consumption. Port of New Orleans is a river gateway strong in breakbulk rather than a container alternative to Savannah or Norfolk.

Ocean rate comparisons between US container ports on opposite coasts are the most common mistake in this cluster, and the second most common is comparing coasts without naming a destination city — 16 of the 183 pages SeaRates measured on 07/26/2026 make the first one. A precise inland comparison — Chicago, Dallas, Memphis or Atlanta against competing entry ports, with rail transit and cost — is a separate, per-market question from the coast-level one answered here: how the gateways into this market compare runs it for Chicago.

Port of entry, port of discharge, FIRMS code and CBP port code are four different things

A US port of entry, a port of discharge, a FIRMS code and a CBP port code are 4 different identifiers that importers routinely treat as one, and exactly 1 of the 183 pages SeaRates measured on 07/26/2026 explains the difference between the first two. Getting them confused puts the wrong facility on an entry and holds the cargo at the terminal.

Port of entry vs port of discharge is the pairing importers confuse most often, and the two coincide most of the time and diverge whenever cargo moves in bond. A container discharged at Norfolk and moved in bond to Columbus is discharged in Virginia and entered in Ohio, and the entry names the inland port. Bills of lading name the discharge port, so reading the entry port off a bill of lading is a common and expensive substitution.

A FIRMS code names the shed inside the port, not the city: the Facilities Information and Resources Management System code is assigned to a bonded warehouse, container freight station or foreign trade zone once that facility has filed its bond with CBP, and it tells the broker where the goods physically are. FIRMS codes appear on 6 of the 183 pages measured, which is why arrival notices carrying one are so often forwarded to brokers without comment.

CBP publishes the current port code list itself and revises it, so a code copied from a blog post is a code with an unknown date. Pull port codes and FIRMS codes from CBP directly on the day the entry is filed, and check that the FIRMS code on the arrival notice matches the facility the trucker is actually going to.

Port congestion in 2026 and the suspended USTR port fees

US port congestion in 2026 is a yard and dwell problem rather than the anchorage queues of 2021, and the special port fees that dominated the 2025 headlines are suspended until 11/09/2026 — 106 days after this page was measured on 07/26/2026. Two separate things are therefore in play, and only one of them touches a container invoice today.

US Trade Representative port fees apply to vessels rather than to boxes, and the published schedule sets three categories: $50 per net ton per US voyage for Chinese-owned or operated vessels under Annex I, the higher of $18 per net ton or $120 per container for Chinese-built vessels under Annex II, and $150 per Car-Equivalent Unit for foreign-built vehicle carriers under Annex III. Collection is suspended from 11/10/2025 through 11/09/2026 under the US-China truce, so no USTR special fee is being collected at any US port inside that window. How much of the fee carriers passed through to container surcharges before the suspension is not stated in any source available at the time of writing, and no figure for it appears here.

Port congestion in 2026 shows up as yard density and long dwell rather than as ships waiting offshore. Vessel waiting time at Los Angeles ran around one day in mid-June 2026 by carrier port updates, while yard dwell at Savannah was reported around 9.1 days in early July 2026. Smaller gateways absorb some of that pressure: Port Hueneme, the Port of Baltimore and Port Houston were named as the least congested US options in Google's AI Overview captured on 07/25/2026, and a congestion tracker put New York at 3 days, Savannah at 2 and Los Angeles at 2 in the same week.

Port congestion figures carry the shortest shelf life of anything on this page, and SeaRates re-reads them at every refresh rather than carrying them forward. Treat any congestion number older than a month as history, and read the port's own dashboard before committing a booking.

What goes wrong at a US container port, and what each failure costs

Five failures account for most of the money importers lose at a US container port, and 4 of the 5 are timing failures rather than pricing failures. Each one has a specific price and a specific counterparty.

  • The last free day passes at the terminal. Demurrage in North America averages about $138 per container per day with a common range of $100 to $300, which makes a four-day slip on a single box a four-figure event.
  • The port adds its own dwell fee on top. Port Houston assesses a Sustained Import Dwell Fee of $45 per unit per day starting on the eighth day after free time expires, and bills it directly to the beneficial cargo owner rather than to the carrier or the forwarder.
  • The trucker arrives and the box is not available. A dry run is billed in full, the appointment is lost, and the container often waits another day for the next slot.
  • The chassis is not where the container is. A chassis split turns one move into two and is not in the original dray quote; chassis rental then runs per day until the box is returned.
  • The rail ramp, not the terminal, is the bottleneck. Rail-destined containers at Los Angeles and Long Beach averaged 5.06 days in April 2026, so an intermodal booking can burn free time while the box has never left the port.

A demurrage invoice from a terminal or a carrier can also fail on its own terms. Under the Federal Maritime Commission rule effective 05/28/2024, a demurrage or detention invoice must be issued within 30 calendar days of the charge accruing, must go to the billed party or the consignee but not to both, and must contain a defined set of elements; an invoice missing any required element ends the obligation to pay it, and the recipient has at least 30 days to dispute. Reading the invoice against that list before paying is free, and 5 of the 183 pages SeaRates measured on 07/26/2026 mention the billing rule at all.

Demurrage runs while the box sits inside the terminal; detention runs while you hold the carrier's box outside it. Confusing the two produces disputes with the wrong counterparty, and the two clocks can run at the same time on the same container — the SeaRates how the container lines compare on capacity and schedule reliability sets out which party bills which clock.

Which US port is cheapest to import through?

No single US port is cheapest for everyone. The cheapest gateway is the one where terminal handling, drayage, free time and inland transit sum to the least for one destination and one box size, and the ocean rate rarely decides it.

What is the busiest container port in the United States?

The Port of Los Angeles handles the largest container volume: 1,002,734 TEUs in June 2026 and 5,122,603 TEUs in the first half of 2026, published by the port on 07/15/2026.

What are the top US container ports by volume?

Los Angeles, Long Beach and New York and New Jersey lead by published container throughput, followed by Savannah, Houston, Virginia, Charleston, Oakland and the Northwest Seaport Alliance. Rankings shift with the period measured, so check the publication date.

Which US port has the shortest dwell time?

No US port holds that position permanently. Savannah and New York and New Jersey are frequently among the most fluid large gateways, and the Port of Virginia reports rail-ready import dwell of 40 to 45 hours; figures change weekly with trucking and rail capacity.

What is a dwell time fee?

A dwell time fee is charged by a port authority for containers left on terminal beyond free time. Port Houston assesses a Sustained Import Dwell Fee of $45 per unit per day from the eighth day after free time expires, billed to the cargo owner.

How many free days do containers get at US ports?

Free time in US import trades commonly runs 3 to 7 days, granted by the ocean carrier or the terminal rather than by the port. Standard imports at Savannah are generally granted 4 to 5 days under Georgia Ports Authority rules.

When does free time start counting?

The start hour comes from the carrier's or terminal's tariff. Georgia Ports Authority counts from the first 12:01 AM after unloading, Maersk counts Los Angeles and Long Beach imports from the first 3:00 AM after discharge, and Seaboard Marine counts from 08:00 on the first business day after discharge.

What does drayage cost at a US port?

Local drayage from Los Angeles and Long Beach was quoted at roughly $400 to $900 per container in 2026 by vendor sources, before a fuel surcharge of 15% to 20%. Comparable published rates across US ports do not exist, so quote per destination ZIP code.

Why is drayage so expensive?

Drayage prices short moves at a port with long unproductive time: gate queues, chassis collection, appointment windows and waiting. A chassis split, a pre-pull or a dry run each adds a move that was not in the base rate.

What is a terminal handling charge?

A terminal handling charge is the carrier's charge for moving a container across the terminal. Hapag-Lloyd's North America schedule prices Baltimore at USD 600 for a 20-foot dry box and USD 750 for a 40-foot dry box; amounts differ by carrier and by port.

Who pays the terminal handling charge?

The party named on the freight terms pays it. On a destination-terms shipment the consignee pays the destination terminal handling charge, which is why it appears on the arrival invoice rather than in the ocean quote.

What is the difference between the Port of Los Angeles and the Port of Long Beach?

Two separate municipal authorities share San Pedro Bay: the Los Angeles Harbor Department runs one, the Long Beach Harbor Department the other. They compete for vessel calls, publish volumes separately, and run different truck programmes.

Is Savannah bigger than Charleston?

The Port of Savannah handles substantially more container volume, at nearly 5.7 million TEUs in calendar 2025. Charleston carries a deeper 52-foot harbor and a different cargo mix weighted to automotive, aerospace and project cargo.

Which port is best for imports from China?

Destination decides it. Los Angeles and Long Beach carry the shortest ocean transit from Asia and the deepest sailing schedules; New York and New Jersey or Savannah cut the inland leg for eastern destinations.

What is the difference between a port of entry and a port of discharge?

A port of entry is the government-designated place where goods legally enter the United States and customs entry is filed. A port of discharge is where the cargo is lifted off the vessel. In-bond moves separate the two.

What is a FIRMS code?

A FIRMS code is a four-character alphanumeric identifier assigned by US Customs and Border Protection to a specific facility handling imported cargo — a bonded warehouse, container freight station or foreign trade zone — after that facility files its bond.

Where do I find CBP port codes?

US Customs and Border Protection publishes the port code list and revises it. Take the code from CBP on the day the entry is filed rather than from a third-party page of unknown date.

Are US port fees being charged in 2026?

US Trade Representative special port fees on vessels with a Chinese nexus are suspended from 11/10/2025 through 11/09/2026. Ordinary port authority tariffs — dockage, wharfage, storage — continue unchanged.

Which US ports have the least congestion?

Port Hueneme, the Port of Baltimore and Port Houston were named as the least congested US gateways in Google's AI Overview captured on 07/25/2026. Congestion figures change weekly, so read the port's own dashboard before booking.

Do Seattle and Tacoma compete for my cargo?

No. Marine cargo operations at Seattle and Tacoma merged into the Northwest Seaport Alliance in 2015, which handled 262,546 TEUs in June 2026 and routes a vessel to whichever harbor suits the call.

Does the entry port change the federal fees I pay?

No. The harbor maintenance fee and the merchandise processing fee are federal and calculated on cargo value, identically at every US port. Terminal handling, storage and drayage are the lines that change.
