Door-to-door, port pickup or port drop-off

Barrel service levels differ by liability before they differ by price: port drop-off is the cheapest and leaves clearance to you, which makes the three options a liability question before they are a price question. Barrel shipping companies use the same three words for the same three boundaries.

Door-to-door, port pickup or port drop-off
ServiceSender doesReceiver does
Door-to-doorHands it over at homeWaits at the delivery address
Port pickupDrops it at the agentCollects from the warehouse
Port drop-offDrops it at the agentClears customs personally

A barrel is a shipping unit priced whole rather than by weight, which is why the service level moves the total more than the contents do. Service scope is named on 48 pages of this corpus and priced separately on almost none. Two identical shipments on the same sailing, one door-to-door and one drop-off, differ in price by the cost of two local operations, not by anything that happens at sea. Households moving in full rather than sending barrels are priced by volume instead, and what shipping a full container of household goods costs works in cubic metres rather than in units.

How long a barrel takes, and why the advertised figure is not a promise

Barrel transit is quoted by 11 of 48 pages and the figures do not agree with each other. Shipping to the Caribbean runs on consolidation: the unit waits for the container to fill, the container waits for the sailing, and the sailing waits for nothing.

SeaRates has not measured a transit median for these corridors, so the ranges advertised elsewhere appear here as claims rather than findings. Shipments booked against a fixed date — a birthday, a funeral, Christmas — need the cut-off from the operator in writing, because the published figure describes a good week rather than a typical one. The US end of the transit is measurable even where the corridor is not: which US port is cheapest to import through covers dwell and charges at the terminal the barrel leaves from.

How to pack a barrel that survives the examination

Jamaican law requires the importer to unpack and repack the barrel personally. Section 227 of the Customs Act puts the work on the owner: the officer directs it to be opened for examination, and the person clearing it does the opening, the unloading and the repacking, standing at the counter.

Packing order therefore has a cost in your own time:

  1. Pack the barrel in layers you can rebuild. Whatever comes out has to go back in, by you, on a warehouse floor.
  2. Keep a written list matching the layers. An officer with a list samples; an officer without one keeps going.
  3. Separate anything that looks commercial. Twelve identical items read as stock rather than as gifts.
  4. Put fragile items where lifting them out is easy, not where they are best protected in transit.

Dimensions are named by only 5 of 48 pages measured on 07/26/2026, which means the unit everybody prices is rarely the unit anybody defines.

What happens when the barrel lands in Jamaica

Personal effects in a barrel attract a flat JMD$6,500 charge on entry to Jamaica, and the Jamaica Customs Agency publishes that figure itself, with the note that the rate is subject to change. A flat charge is unusual in freight and it matters here: it means the arithmetic for a household consignment does not depend on what the officer thinks the contents are worth, provided the contents stay non-commercial.

Clearing a barrel on somebody else's behalf adds a requirement that catches families out: an authorisation letter signed and stamped by a Justice of the Peace, together with the importer's TRN card and identification, and photocopies stamped by a JP as well. Travellers clearing their own consignment bring a passport or the landing slip from the airport kiosk.

Returning resident relief in Jamaica has not been read at source by SeaRates. The agency publishes a Returning Residents section, and 6 of 48 competing pages mention such relief, but the conditions are not captured here and are not summarised from secondary sources.

Is the shipper licensed for the ocean leg?

Barrel shipping companies rarely name a regulator: one of 48 pages ranking here mentions the licence that covers the ocean leg. Barrel shipping sits in the part of the market where the top results are Yelp listings, Facebook groups and a freight marketplace — venues built to introduce parties rather than to verify them.

An FMC Ocean Transportation Intermediary licence covers arranging the ocean carriage; a USDOT number, which none of the 48 pages mentions either, covers a truck moving between US states. Operators subcontract the water, so the name on the receipt and the name on the bill of lading need not match.

Barrel senders settle the question in one call: ask which company issues the bill of lading, then look that name up rather than the storefront's. The check runs on the same registry as the licence and bond behind the company name, and operators that pass it appear in how international movers compare on our scorecard.

Drums win on volume per dollar and lose on flexibility: the unit is fixed, and a half-empty one costs what a full one does. Boxes scale down cleanly, which matters when the shipment is a few items rather than a household's worth of goods. Senders comparing the two on a household move should start from moving your household goods overseas; those sending to the Philippines meet a separate market again, and how balikbayan box companies compare on rate and transit covers its own operators and its own customs exemption, distinct from Caribbean barrel shipping. Senders shipping a vehicle alongside the barrels meet a third rule set again: what sets the bill for shipping a vehicle overseas covers the 72-hour export filing that personal effects never touch.

What goes wrong: Christmas cut-offs, valuation and storage

Barrels sitting at the bonded warehouse accrue storage while paperwork is being fixed, and the clock does not care whose mistake it was. Shipping to these corridors fails in a small number of predictable ways:

  • The Christmas sailing is missed. Cut-offs are mentioned by 3 of 48 pages and dated by none; get the date from the operator in writing.
  • Contents are valued above expectation. The flat rate covers personal effects and non-commercial goods; anything reading as stock is assessed differently, and how that assessment works has not been captured here.
  • Nobody is authorised to clear. Without the JP-stamped letter and the importer's TRN, the barrel waits and storage accrues.
  • The receiver cannot lift the contents. Somebody has to unpack and repack it personally, which is a practical constraint when the named receiver is elderly.

Cheap to send, expensive to leave sitting.

How much is customs duty on a barrel in Jamaica?

Barrels containing personal effects or non-commercial items attract a flat charge of JMD$6,500, published by the Jamaica Customs Agency and marked as subject to change. Commercial contents are assessed differently, and SeaRates has not captured that calculation.

Do I have to unpack the barrel myself?

Yes. Section 227 of the Jamaican Customs Act requires the importer to unpack and repack their own shipment; the Customs Officer directs the examination but does not do the handling.

Can someone else clear my barrel?

Yes, with an authorisation letter signed and stamped by a Justice of the Peace, plus the importer's Tax Registration Number card and valid identification. Photocopied documents must also be signed and stamped by a JP.
