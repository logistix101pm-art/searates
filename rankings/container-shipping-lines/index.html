What container shipping rankings measure, and which carrier actually keeps its schedule

By SeaRates · Updated 29 July 2026 · Every figure carries its unit, its reading date and a linked source · Send a correction

⚑ Two measurements, one phrase

Container shipping companies rankings answer three different questions with one list, and the three answers do not agree with each other. What the published measurements actually say:

  • Capacity is what the carrier league table ranks. Alphaliner's TOP 100 put the world fleet at 7,615 active ships and 34,516,221 TEU on 25.07.2026, with MSC holding 21.4% of it.
  • Reliability is a different publisher and a different order. Sea-Intelligence put May 2026 at 64.7% on time industry-wide, with Maersk at 78.2% and Hapag-Lloyd at 76.0% — an ordering that does not match the capacity one.
  • The reliability figure itself is disputed. A Kuehne+Nagel summary gives June 2026 at 56.6% and names April the best month at 62.4%. April is the only month the two sources agree on.
  • Price is a third measurement again. Drewry's World Container Index stood at $4,374 per 40-foot container on 07/25/2026, down 4% in a week.
  • Rank is partly rented. ONE runs 53.1% of its capacity on chartered ships, COSCO 39.6% and Hapag-Lloyd 39.4% — tonnage the operator can hand back.
  • Almost nobody says what they are ranking by. Of the 34 pages ranking for these queries, 11 name the metric, 2 show a score, 2 publish a method and none reports a sample size.
  • SeaRates publishes no score of its own here, because a useful one would have to be lane-specific and that data has not been collected.

Which carrier keeps its schedule

Maersk was the most reliable top-13 carrier in May 2026 at 78.2%, followed by Hapag-Lloyd at 76.0% and MSC at 71.6%. Sea-Intelligence measured global schedule reliability across 34 trade lanes and more than 60 carriers, putting the industry figure at 64.7% for the month — the highest of 2026 and still 1.2 points below the same month a year earlier.

Where the reliability figure itself disagrees

Container shipping companies rankings inherit a problem their sources have: the same period gets different numbers. Sea-Intelligence, publishing the Global Liner Performance report, puts May 2026 at 64.7% and calls it the best month of the year. A Kuehne+Nagel seaexplorer summary carried into this project gives June 2026 at 56.6% and calls April the best month at 62.4%.

April is the one month both agree on, at 62.4%. They disagree on May and on which month leads the year, which can happen when publishers count different vessel sets or different definitions of on-time. SeaRates publishes both figures with their sources rather than choosing one, and a reader comparing carriers should check which report a number came from before treating it as fact.

Rate indices are a third measurement again

Container shipping companies rankings price nothing at all, and a third publisher fills the gap: Drewry's World Container Index stood at $4,374 per 40-foot container on 07/25/2026, down 4% in a week. Ocean carriers are ranked by capacity, measured by reliability and priced by an index — three publishers, three units, and no page in this corpus that connects them.

Ocean carriers get priced more carefully than they get ranked: 17 of 34 pages quote a figure and 10 date it — 29.4%, the highest rate of dated pricing SeaRates has measured in any cluster. Index journalism has the habit that ranking journalism lacks: it prints the week next to the number, because a spot rate without a week is worthless.

The Drewry index is a market average and not a quote, and ocean carriers do not sell at it. What an individual importer pays depends on volume, contract status and the forwarder in between, which is why the rate question is a different question from the carrier question: what the delivered container actually bills takes it line by line.

When the carrier's identity actually matters to you

Container shipping companies rankings never say who you will actually contract with: 2 pages of 34 mention carrier licensing at all, and none checks a company against a registry. Lines are booked by forwarders and NVOCCs far more often than by shippers directly, and most importers never sign anything with the operator whose ship carries the box. A VOCC owns or operates vessels; an NVOCC issues its own bill of lading and is licensed by the FMC; a forwarder arranges carriage as your agent.

Carriers and intermediaries differ in three practical ways: whose bill of lading governs the cargo, whose service contract the rate came from, and who is liable when the container is late or damaged. Ask which line the booking is with and which document you will receive — the answers are on the booking confirmation, and a counterparty who cannot produce them is not one to rely on.

Where an intermediary sits between you and the carrier, its credential is checkable: what the FMC registry says about this company covers how.

Whether the carrier's box is yours alone or shared changes both the liability and the arithmetic, and where the LCL saving stops sets the crossover against the destination charges.

What you can do when a carrier bills you anyway

Under the FMC final rule in force since 28.05.2024, a carrier's demurrage or detention invoice must be issued within 30 calendar days of the charge arising, must go to the contracting party or the consignee but not both, and loses its force if it omits any required element. The recipient has at least 30 days to dispute it.

Ocean carriers rank badly or well on schedule, but the bill that actually reaches an importer is usually about time at the terminal rather than time at sea. That rule is the one enforceable lever a cargo owner holds without going to court, and how the demurrage and detention clock runs sets out the mechanics.

Those credentials go unmentioned by 32 of 34 pages in this corpus, and no page checks a company against a registry. Complaint data with a shipment count behind it appears nowhere, so no complaint ranking appears here either.

A carrier's demurrage or detention invoice has to clear four tests under that rule:

  • Timing. Issued within 30 calendar days of the charge arising.
  • Addressee. Sent to the contracting party or to the consignee, and not to both.
  • Completeness. Missing any required element, it loses its force.
  • Your window. At least 30 days to dispute it from the date of issue.

Three of the four are checkable from the invoice itself, before any argument about whether the delay was anyone's fault.

Ocean carriers get searched for beside the words scams, complaints, ratings and to avoid, and the honest answer is that three of those four point at a different party. A container line is not an intermediary: it files tariffs rather than holding an OTI licence, so there is no registry row to look up and no bond standing behind it. What can be checked about a line is narrow and public — its schedule reliability from a named publisher with the month attached, and whether the demurrage invoice it sends meets the four tests above.

Ocean carriers are also rarely the counterparty in a fraud complaint. The party that took the booking is, and that one is checkable: an NVOCC or a forwarder appears in the federal register with a licence number and a bond, which is the check set out under what the FMC registry says about this company. Ratings in the sense the query means do not exist here either — no publisher scores container lines on service the way review platforms score retail firms, and a complaint count without a shipment denominator would not be a rating if it did. Capacity, reliability and orderbook are what can be ordered honestly, which is why this page orders those three and labels each by what it counts.

Why do rankings of container shipping companies differ?

Because they measure different things: Alphaliner ranks capacity, Sea-Intelligence measures on-time performance, Drewry prices the spot market. A page that reproduces one table without naming the metric produces an order that cannot be checked.

Can I book directly with a container line?

Usually only with volume and a service contract. Most importers book through a forwarder or an NVOCC, which means the bill of lading and the liability may sit with the intermediary rather than with the carrier whose ship you can see.

What does a high orderbook share mean?

That the operator has ordered a lot of new capacity relative to what it runs today — COSCO 45.2%, CMA CGM 39.8%, MSC 35.4%. Orderbooks change the market's balance in future years; they say nothing about service now.

What can I do about a demurrage invoice from a carrier?

Check it against the FMC rule in force since 28.05.2024: it must arrive within 30 days of the charge, go to one party rather than two, and contain every required element. A missing element ends the obligation to pay, and you have at least 30 days to dispute.
