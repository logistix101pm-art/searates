Two things follow for international movers from the two rows that are filled in. The bond behind an ocean licence is a published number rather than a claim — $50,000 for a forwarder, $75,000 for an NVOCC — and it is a shared pool across all claimants, not insurance on one household's goods. And the licensed entity is not always the brand on the advertising: the register lists United Van Lines International, Inc., which is a different legal person from the van line whose name the lists carry.

The estimate is where the money is decided

International moving companies rankings say almost nothing about the estimate, and the estimate decides the money: a written one must sit on an inspection, and 1 page of 27 explains what happens when the loaded volume exceeds the survey.

The estimate is where the money is decided
EstimateWhat it guaranteesPayable at deliveryWhere the rule applies
BindingTotal will not exceed the estimate100% of the estimateInterstate moves under FMCSA
Non-bindingNothing; it is a projectionUp to 110% at deliveryInterstate moves under FMCSA
International ocean quoteWhatever the contract saysPer the contractOutside the FMCSA rules

Movers are told plainly by the road regulator that a rate quote is not an estimate and that an estimate must rest on an actual or virtual inspection. A binding estimate guarantees the delivery bill does not exceed it; under a non-binding one the mover may not demand more than 110% at delivery, and the balance is billed afterwards and still owed.

Estimate rules of that kind bind interstate movers, and an international ocean shipment sits outside them — which is exactly why a household should insist on the same standard by contract. A moving company quoting a household by telephone, without seeing the rooms, is quoting a number it can revise at loading. How the volume itself is measured and priced is set out in moving your household goods overseas.

Deposit, hostage load and the complaint nobody counts

International moving companies rankings rarely reach the part where deals break: 12 of 27 pages discuss deposits, 3 mention held shipments, and 4 publish any complaint data. Movers fail in a small number of recognisable ways, and each is visible before the truck arrives.

  • The volume re-price. A mover quoting without a survey issues a larger invoice once the crew measures what is actually loaded.
  • The hostage load. A disputed invoice becomes leverage the moment the household's belongings are in a warehouse, and 3 of 27 pages in this corpus name the pattern.
  • The broker with no assets. The company that quoted subcontracts the whole move, and the crew that arrives has no contract with the household at all.
  • The deposit that outlives the service. A large non-refundable deposit taken before any booking exists is a signal, not a formality.

A complaint count means nothing without the number of moves behind it: two complaints across four hundred moves and two across nine are not comparable, so no complaint league table appears here. Costs that arrive after the quote — duty, terminal handling, storage — are set out in the landed cost calculator, and households sending goods without moving themselves should compare what the barrel shippers on this corridor charge instead.

Moving companies fail in ways that reviews and forum threads file under scams, and the word flattens four different things. Three of the four patterns above are contract design rather than fraud: a re-price that follows an estimate made without a survey, a deposit taken before any booking exists, and a crew that arrives holding no contract with the household. The fourth, a held load, is the one that converts a billing dispute into leverage. All four are visible before the truck arrives, which is why this page ranks what a household can check rather than publishing a list of movers to avoid.

What are red flags for moving companies?

A price given without a survey, a large non-refundable deposit before any booking exists, no named entity on the bill of lading, and an unwillingness to say who performs the ocean leg. Each is checkable in one conversation.

How can I avoid being scammed by a moving company?

Insist on a written estimate based on an inspection, confirm which legal entity issues the bill of lading, and look that entity up in the FMC registry. A mover unable to complete those three steps is not yet worth a shortlist place.

Is International Van Lines a reputable moving company?

SeaRates publishes no score for it or for anyone else on this page. What a household can check for any named company is identical: road authority, the FMC status of whoever carries the ocean leg, current FAIM accreditation, and whether the estimate followed a survey.

What is a commonly overlooked cost when moving abroad?

Destination-side charges: terminal handling, customs clearance, the local agent, storage after free time, and delivery above the ground floor. None of them appears in an ocean quote, and duty sits outside all of them.

Do FIDI or FAIM accreditation guarantee quality?

No. FAIM audits process, finances and insurance on a cycle, which is more than a review average does and less than a performance guarantee. Accreditation narrows a shortlist; it does not price a move or promise a transit time.
