Broker, forwarder, NVOCC or vessel line: who owes you what

Car shipping companies stand in four legally different positions, and 4 of 27 pages explain the difference between a broker and the carrier that signs the bill of lading. Car shipping companies advertise the same service and stand in four legally different positions, and the position decides who answers when the car is damaged, delayed or held.

An auto transport broker arranges the move and subcontracts it to a carrier, and its FMCSA authority covers the US road leg only. An NVOCC issues its own bill of lading and takes carrier liability with it. An ocean freight forwarder books space in your name without issuing a document of its own.

The $75,000 NVOCC bond is a shared pool across all claimants, not insurance on one car. Where the method of shipment matters to the choice — a running car on a RoRo deck against a project car in a container — the decision belongs upstream, in whether the car travels RoRo or in a container.

Where the deal breaks: deposit, resold booking, disputed damage

Ten of 27 pages discuss deposits and upfront payment, and three publish any complaint data at all. Car shipping companies rarely fail spectacularly; the ordinary failures are three, and each is visible before the money moves.

  • The resold booking. An auto transport broker takes the deposit and passes the job to a company you never chose. The second firm has no relationship with you and no reason to honour the first one's promises.
  • The deposit outliving the service. A large non-refundable deposit taken before a vessel is booked is a signal, not a formality: ask which sailing the booking is against and what happens to the money if it does not exist.
  • The disputed damage. Photographs at drop-off and the terminal's own condition report are the only records an insurer compares against arrival. Without them, damage is undated and undated damage is nobody's.

A complaint count means nothing without the number of shipments behind it. Two complaints across four hundred moves and two across nine are not comparable, so no complaint league table appears here: SeaRates has no denominator for any company, and neither does any page in the corpus. Costs that arrive after the quote — duty, port charges, clearance — are set out in the full landed cost breakdown, duty included.

The three patterns above have four free federal checks against them, and the order they run in matters: how not to get scammed shipping a car takes about nine minutes end to end.

Car shipping companies are searched for beside the words scams, ratings, most reliable and to avoid, and those four words want three different things. Two of the three patterns above are contract design rather than fraud — a broker reselling a booking it never owned, and a deposit taken before a sailing exists — while the third, damage nobody will date, is a records problem. Outright fraud is the rarest of them and the easiest to screen for, because it fails the first of the three checks below: a company that will not name the legal person issuing the bill of lading has already answered the question.

Ratings in the sense the query means do not exist for this market. 12 pages of the 27 rank on platform reviews and one of those twelve says how it weighs them, and a review score measures a customer's dealings with a road broker rather than the performance of the ocean leg. A most-reliable ordering would need a complaint record with a shipment denominator behind it, which no page in this corpus holds and neither does SeaRates. What exists instead is the licence, the role and the document — checkable in about five minutes, and none of the three is an opinion.

Three things you can check before paying a deposit

Three of 27 pages check a company against a registry, and none links to the FMC list. Car shipping companies can be screened in about five minutes with three questions, none of which requires a ranking.

  1. Which legal entity issues the bill of lading? Car shipping companies trade under names, and a name is not a licence. The entity on the document is the one a claim goes to, and it is often not the brand on the website.
  2. Does that entity appear in the FMC registry? The registry held 3,273 licensed ocean freight forwarder records and 9,030 NVOCC records when SeaRates read it on 07/26/2026; the NVOCC list mixes licensed operators with registered foreign ones, so read the licence column rather than the row count. The step-by-step version is on which credential covers the ocean leg.
  3. Can the company explain the export filing? Census requires EEI through AES 72 hours before export for every used self-propelled vehicle regardless of destination or value. The ITN comes back per VIN and can be requested. A company unable to explain the filing is unlikely to be doing it.

Country-specific admission rules — age limits, drive side, duty — sit outside these rankings, and the corridor pages that would carry them are not published yet. The US-side export duties that apply whatever the destination are set out under the two 72-hour filings and what each one wants.

Frequently asked questions

Ask which legal entity issues the bill of lading, check that entity in the FMC registry, and ask the company to explain the 72-hour export filing. A firm answering all three clearly is not a scam; a firm unable to answer the first is not yet a counterparty. No page in this search result answers that verifiably, and neither does this one.

How do I not get scammed shipping my car?

Ask which legal entity issues the bill of lading, check that entity in the FMC registry, and ask the company to explain the 72-hour export filing. A firm answering all three clearly is not a scam; a firm unable to answer the first is not yet a counterparty.

What is the most reputable car shipping company?

No page in this search result answers that verifiably, and neither does this one. Reputation here is measured by review platforms, which record how a broker communicates rather than whether the ocean leg was performed. Compare licence status, role and quote completeness instead.

How to tell if a car shipping company is legit?

Three checks: the entity on the bill of lading, its presence in the FMC OTI registry, and its account of the AES filing. A USDOT number covers US trucking only and does not answer the ocean question.

Can you negotiate with car shipping companies?

With a broker, often — the margin is theirs to move. With a vessel operator, rarely, because the tariff is filed. What is always negotiable is the scope: which charges are included, who pays destination handling, and when the deposit becomes non-refundable.

Do cars get damaged during shipping?

Some do. What decides a claim is documentation: photographs at drop-off, the terminal condition report, and the arrival survey. Damage recorded at both ends is payable; damage that appears only at delivery rarely is.

Which is better, Sherpa or Montway?

Both are US brokers, which means the comparison is between two intermediaries rather than between two carriers. Ask each which ocean partner they book through, whether that partner holds an FMC licence, and who signs the bill of lading.
